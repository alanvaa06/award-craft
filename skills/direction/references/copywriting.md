# Copywriting Doctrine

Lookup reference for an agent writing or judging landing-page copy — page
architecture, hero tests, narrative arc, voice of customer, awareness/
sophistication, headline craft, micro-copy, POV, and banned language, plus the
documented gaps. Distilled from adversarially verified doctrine (2026-08-07
run: 22 sources, 109 claims extracted, 25 verified 3 votes each -> 12
findings, 3 refuted). Nothing below originates in the 2024-2026 window —
Ogilvy (1963-83), Schwartz (1966), Wiebe/Unbounce (~2012-15), and Shapiro
(~2019-21) supply nearly everything that survived verification.

## 1. Page Architecture

- **One goal, one CTA** — repeatable on-page, never competing. "Every landing
  page should have one goal. Only one goal... That one CTA can be repeated on
  the page." 2026 practitioner benchmarks put one-CTA pages ~20-30% ahead of
  competing-CTA pages.
- **Each element has exactly one job**: the headline keeps the visitor on the
  page, the subhead moves them into the body, the button gets clicked.
- **Proven scroll order** (Shapiro): navbar -> hero (header + subheader +
  imagery) -> social proof -> CTA with incentive -> 3-6 feature/objection
  blocks -> repeat CTA -> footer. Scope caveat from the source itself — a
  strongly recommended template, not law: "the more you detour from the
  template, the more confused the average visitor becomes."
- The hero carries a **message-matched USP** echoing whatever drove the click.

## 2. The Hero — Three Operational Tests

| Test | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Grunt test** (Miller, *Building a StoryBrand*) | Within ~5 seconds the first screen answers: what do you offer, how does it make my life better, what do I do to buy it |
| **Descriptiveness test** (Shapiro) | "The header must be fully descriptive of what you're selling" — reading only the header, does the visitor know exactly what is sold? |
| **Subheader job** | One to two sentences, fixed job: state what the product is, then explain how the header's bold claim is possible |

**Hook sources**: a bold specific claim, or a key objection addressed head-on.

**Tension worth naming**: the descriptiveness "must" is strongest for
unknown products. Established premium brands routinely ship evocative heroes
that fail it — which is exactly the gap between conversion doctrine and
award-site practice. Resolve it per project, don't pretend it isn't there.

## 3. Narrative Arc — PAS

**Problem-Agitation-Solution** is Wiebe's most-recommended framework ("my
very favorite framework without question"). The agitation phase is an
**ordered 8-step sequence** mapped directly onto scroll sections, each step
becoming a crosshead:

1. State the big problem
2. Show how it manifests (work / mirror / mind / community / house / bank account)
3. Explain why it exists
4. Name the misconceptions
5. Detail the costs
6. Address failed solutions
7. Explain why they can't solve it alone
8. Paint the improved outcome

**Order matters** — this is the page flow, not a menu to pick from.

### Refuted (0-3) — do not use

- A fixed **seven-section StoryBrand scroll order** (Hero -> Stakes -> Value
  Prop -> Guide -> Offers -> Plan -> Transitional CTA). Not a required
  structure.
- A **mandatory "Stakes" section** naming the cost of inaction.
  Tension-building is a technique, not a required section.
- "The headline's sole job is momentum, not selling." The headline sells.

## 4. Voice of Customer — the Strongest Single Tactic

Headlines **swiped verbatim from recorded customer language** (testimonials,
reviews, support emails, surveys) beat marketer-invented phrasing.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Keep&Share — testimonial-swiped headline | **+103% clicks**, vs the professionally copywritten headline at **-64%** |
| Beachway — Amazon-review headline ("If you think you need rehab, you do") | ~400% click lift, ~26% significant lead lift over marketer control |

The source generalizes it itself: "Repeatedly, the best-performing messages
are those that are swiped directly from: Testimonials, Customer reviews...
Support emails."

**Caveat**: both are single unreplicated, self-reported tests with no
published sample sizes or statistics. Cite them as attributed data points,
never as universal effect sizes.

For problem-aware traffic, restate the strongest customer problem in the
customer's own recorded wording. Most-aware audiences convert better on
offer/product headlines instead (§5).

**Operational consequence**: mining real customer language is a research
step *before* writing, not a polish pass after. Where no customer corpus
exists, the interview that produces PRODUCT.md is the substitute — record
phrasing, don't paraphrase it.

## 5. Awareness and Sophistication Govern Everything

**Schwartz's five awareness stages** — Most Aware, Product Aware, Solution
Aware, Pain Aware, Unaware — dictate headline strategy *and* page length.

| Stage | Headline strategy |
|---|---|
| Most Aware | Product name + bargain price |
| Solution / Problem Aware | Name the desire, prove satisfaction is possible, show the product contains the mechanism |
| Unaware | Dramatize the need vividly, call the market into the headline, *then* present the product |

**Length method** (verbatim): "Page length... is dictated by the level of
awareness" and "I recommend starting by writing a landing page for those who
are Pain Aware and then cutting sections out..." — draft the longest
Pain-Aware page first, then cut down per higher-awareness segment. Offer
risk and decision complexity also move optimal length.

**Five sophistication stages** (how tired the market is):

| Stage | Move |
|---|---|
| 1 — new market | Simple direct claim |
| 2 | Enlarged claim |
| 3 | New mechanism — shift from *what* to *how* |
| 4 | Elaborated mechanism |
| 5 — exhausted | Abandon desire claims, tap **identification** |

**Agent rule for crowded categories: lead with mechanism, not louder benefit
claims.**

## 6. Headline Craft — Ogilvy and Schwartz

**Ogilvy**: promise a benefit, be **specific** — percentages, time elapsed,
dollars saved. Never clever, tricky, or adjective-laden.

The famous "5x as many read the headline as body copy, so a non-selling
headline wastes 90% of the spend" is a mid-century Starch/Gallup-era
heuristic with no locatable dataset. Attribute it to Ogilvy; never present
it as current measurement.

**Schwartz's 38 claim-intensification techniques** (*Breakthrough
Advertising* Ch. 4) work as a selection menu: measure the size or speed of
the claim, state it as paradox, connect mechanism to claim, name the
problem, turn the claim into a challenge, and so on. Technique choice is
conditioned on audience state — mechanism-led when *how it works* is what
builds belief.

**Precision rule**: oddly specific figures beat round numbers for proof
claims — `$47,382` over `$50,000`, `13 days` over `2 weeks`. Consumers were
significantly more skeptical of "50% longer" than of "47%" or "53%";
precise numbers read as research-based (Schindler & Yalch). **Exception**:
round *prices* can outperform precise ones for hedonic, feeling-driven
purchases (Wadhwa & Zhang 2015, JCR).

## 7. Micro-copy — Documented Tests

| Rule | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Preview the post-click result, reduce anxiety | "Show me my quotes" beat "Get my quotes" by **+10.3%** |
| Never remind users of upcoming work | "Add It to WordPress Now" cut CTR **-15%** and produced fewer signups than "Try It Free Now" |
| Echo the headline's verb-phrase in the button | **+24% clicks** when button copy matched headline copy |

Use first-person possessive ("my") framing in buttons. Source rationale:
"minimize the risk associated with clicking."

**Caveat**: all three are single unreplicated tests (~2014-15) with no
published sample sizes or p-values. In the "Show me" test, first person was
constant across variants — the isolated variable was result-preview vs
ambiguous verb, not grammatical person. Cite as attributed data points,
never as universal effect sizes.

## 8. POV and Benefit-over-Feature

Write body copy in **second person singular**, one human being to another,
telling the reader what the product will do *for them*, with specifics.
Ogilvy: "Pretend you are writing each of them a letter... One human being to
another, second person singular."

This **coexists with first-person buttons** — the first-person A/B wins
apply to button micro-copy only.

## 9. Banned Language

- **Brag and boast** — superlative self-praise ("our product is the best in
  the world"). Stick to verifiable facts about what the product is and does.
  Ogilvy's rule, grounded in Gallup readership research.
- **Vague slogan headers** — "Improve your workflow!", "Supercharge your
  collaboration!", "Empower your sales", "Revolutionize your workflow".
  Explicitly identified as useless corporate speak; bluntly describe the
  value prop instead.
- Modern CRO corroborates: specific claims beat generic superlatives (~95%
  lift in one VWO-cited test).

**The generative rule** — no exhaustive blacklist survived verification, so
use the test instead of a list: **ban any header a competitor could also
claim**.

## Gaps — no verified doctrine

Four requested sub-topics produced zero surviving claims. Naming them beats
filling them with plausible invention — decide these with the client rather
than invent rules:

1. **LatAm bilingual register for premium Spanish-language brands** — tu/
   usted decisions, false-friend pitfalls, tone register. Three
   Spanish-language sources were fetched; none of their claims cleared
   verification.
2. **Verbal-identity operationalization** — the methodology for compiling 3
   brand-voice adjectives into operational rules (POV, sentence-length
   rhythm, allowed/banned lexicon, punctuation habits) with cross-section
   consistency checks. Nothing documented survived; the practice appears to
   live inside agencies rather than in published work.
3. **A credible 2024-2026 AI-copy-tell list** specific to landing pages
   ("Unlock your potential", "Seamless experience", "Elevate your...").
   Five sources on exactly this topic were fetched; none survived. Only
   Ogilvy/Shapiro-era clichés are defensible.
4. **Per-section word/line budgets** — only the subheader (1-2 sentences)
   and the hero (grunt-test-in-5-seconds) verified. No measured counts for
   feature blocks, testimonials, or footer CTAs.

**Caveats on what did survive**: no claim originates in 2024-2026 (all are
canonical doctrine confirmed as still taught); every A/B percentage is a
single self-reported test without published statistics; several findings
route through secondary blogs whose claims verifiers traced to primary
texts — quote the books, not the blogs.

## Key Takeaways

- One goal, one CTA, one job per element — and a proven scroll order (hero
  -> social proof -> CTA -> 3-6 objection blocks -> repeat CTA) that is a
  template, not a law.
- The hero must pass two tests: the grunt test (offer / benefit / how to buy
  in ~5s) and the descriptiveness test. Evocative heroes are a deliberate
  exception for known brands, not a default.
- PAS with Wiebe's ordered 8-step agitation is the scroll arc that verified.
  The fixed StoryBrand section order and the mandatory Stakes section did
  **not** — they were refuted 0-3.
- Voice-of-customer language is the single strongest headline tactic (+103%
  vs -64% for professionally written copy in the same test). Mine real
  phrasing before writing.
- Awareness stage sets length; sophistication stage sets the move. Draft the
  Pain-Aware long page first, then cut. In crowded markets, lead with
  mechanism.
- Buttons: preview the result, never remind users of work, echo the
  headline. First person in buttons, second person singular in body copy.
- The banned-language test that replaces any list: would a competitor be
  able to claim this exact header? If yes, rewrite.
